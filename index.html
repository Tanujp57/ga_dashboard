01 Establish the baseline

A blue state, by a wide margin.

In 1960, John F. Kennedy carried Georgia by — points over Richard Nixon. The county map shows that statewide result was broad—but the shades also leave room for local variation.

Each county is colored by the Democratic share minus the Republican share. The farther from the pale center, the larger the two-party margin. The map tracks presidential votes, not the outcome of every race on the ballot.

Use the map as a baseline, not as a proxy for county-level Senate or governor results.

02 The first break

Four years later, the map changed dramatically.

In 1964, Barry Goldwater carried Georgia. Between the 1960 and 1964 presidential races, the statewide Democratic-minus-Republican margin moved — points toward Republicans.

The change was not geographically uniform. Focus a county to compare its margins across the two elections. A negative swing means movement toward the Republican side of the margin scale; it does not mean every voter changed parties.

—Goldwater share of the two-party vote in 1964

03 One office at a time

Presidential wins did not flip every office.

Republican presidential wins arrived first. The first Republican U.S. senator of the modern era, Mack Mattingly, won in 1980; Democrats won the seat back in 1986. The longer Republican run developed in the 1990s and 2000s. The map stays on the 1980 presidential vote here; county-level Senate results are not present in this map dataset.

For governor, the handoff came later: Sonny Perdue defeated Roy Barnes in 2002, the first Republican gubernatorial win since Reconstruction. The office timelines below compare reported statewide margins—not a single, uniform realignment clock.

04 Not every county tells the same story

The shift had a geography.

This view subtracts each county’s 1960 presidential margin from its 1964 margin. Counties moving toward Republicans are red; counties moving toward Democrats are blue. The measure is a change in the two-party margin, not a count of people who switched sides.

The scatter below the story links county-level presidential margins to the demographic snapshots already included with the project. Those measures are descriptive, and the available snapshots are 2000, 2010 and 2019—not a continuous time series. They do not explain why a county voted as it did.

County population and demographic estimates are a separate source and year from election results.

05 The bigger picture

A staggered shift—not a straight line.

Presidential politics moved first; Senate and gubernatorial outcomes followed on different schedules. And even after a long Republican run, Georgia remained competitive: Joe Biden carried the state in 2020, and Donald Trump carried it again in 2024.

Sources, definitions & limitations

County election results: The project dataset combines historical presidential county results. Statewide results and source notes are in the original project data. County margins are calculated as 100 × (Democratic votes − Republican votes) ÷ total votes, matching the source array structure.

Demographics: County population estimates in the bundled data are for 2000, 2010 and 2019. For a selected election year, the nearest available snapshot is shown and labeled; years before 2000 use the earliest snapshot.

Statewide results: See the original dashboard's source notes preserved in README.md and the metadata embedded in data.js. Special elections/runoffs appear as their own records where supplied. In selected historical races, party, write-in, third-party or legislative-selection details make the two-party margin an incomplete description.

Survey file: The attached standardized exit-poll/survey CSV is retained with the project, but the original dashboard did not make a demographic voting analysis from it. It is not used here to infer county behavior or causal effects.
